Overview

What this report covers

This report documents the research activity carried out during the first six-month period of the Research Grant, focused entirely on REASONED and, within it, on the currently active research question, RQ1: the robustness of human-video-conditioned manipulation policies. RQ2 and RQ3 are planned for the following reporting periods.

RQ1 · current focus

Robustness to novel tasks

How can we develop a system that is robust to novel tasks: new combinations of skills and objects (RQ1-1), and new spatial configurations with known objects (RQ1-2)?

Main Research Project

Human-Video-Conditioned Multi-Task Imitation Learning

A fundamental goal in robotics is building autonomous systems that can perform multiple manipulation tasks in flexible, dynamic environments. Human-Video Conditioning lets a robot learn a new task simply by watching a human demonstrator perform it: a more direct and intuitive form of task specification than natural-language instructions, since a video encodes not only what has to be done but also how. The central challenge is that the human demonstration cannot be transferred to the robot directly: demonstrator and robot may differ in embodiment, object layout, viewpoint, or environment.

Problem formulation

πθ: Or × Sr × Cd → Ar

The robot policy must map its current observation (Or), its proprioceptive state (Sr), and a video demonstration provided by a human demonstrator (Cd) to a robot action (Ar). This work focuses on the most general case: the demonstration is an unstructured video, with no action labels, segmentation, or kinematic alignment between demonstrator and robot.

Taxonomy

The baselines are organized into five method families (hand-tracking, object-centric, end-to-end, Vision-Language-Action, and World-Action Models), spanning Video-Conditioned, Language-Conditioned, and interleaved image-text-conditioned policies. The Video-Conditioned architectures capture the current state of the art in human-video-conditioned manipulation, while the Language-Conditioned ones are included because they sit at one of the most actively pursued research frontiers in Physical AI and Embodied Intelligence.

Video-Conditioned

Hand-tracking

Explicitly estimates the human hand motion from the demonstration and uses it for planning or policy supervision. In Mimic-Play, a latent planner encodes the hand trajectory into a compact task-level plan that conditions a low-level control policy. Intuitive, but it must bridge the embodiment and environment mismatch between the human demonstrator and the robot.

Video-Conditioned

Object-centric

Instead of reproducing the human motion, the robot identifies the object involved in the task and reproduces or adapts its trajectory: a Conditioned-Object Detector localizes the manipulated item and its destination directly from the video demonstration. This reduces the dependency on the human embodiment, but performance can degrade when object appearance or spatial configuration changes significantly.

Video-Conditioned

End-to-end

Removes the explicit extraction of hand or object trajectories and learns a direct mapping from the robot observation and the video demonstration to the robot action: a task-inference module predicts a structured sequence of waypoints, executed through hand-crafted motor primitives. This minimizes manual engineering, at the cost of a harder learning problem and limited interpretability.

Language-Conditioned

Vision-Language-Action

Within the end-to-end family, VLA models extend a large pretrained vision-language backbone with an action-prediction head. The resulting policies inherit the semantic representations and robustness built up during large-scale pretraining, marking a significant step toward general-purpose robotic policies, though they typically demand large-scale datasets and remain difficult to fine-tune efficiently.

Language-Conditioned

World-Action Model

A latent dynamics encoder is pretrained on large-scale, action-free video, including human demonstrations, to predict future visual states. The resulting learned world dynamics are then adapted to condition the action-prediction head.

Image-Text-Conditioned

Interleave-VLA

An instruction-centric take on robustness: a pretrained VLA (π0) is extended, through a lightweight tokenizer adaptation, to accept interleaved image-text instructions in which task-relevant objects are referenced by cropped images (e.g., “Put [image1] into [image2]”). Grounding objects in images rather than ambiguous text mitigates attentional hallucinations in text-only VLAs.

Evaluation scenario: the robot must pick a colored object and place it into the bin indicated by the human video demonstration, across 16 pick-place variations in total. Metrics: Reach, Pick, Success, Success on Wrong Object, and Success on Wrong Bin.

Hand-tracking baseline: Mimic-Play

Mimic-Play was trained under three increasingly challenging settings, progressively widening the trajectory space the latent planner must represent: a fixed shared region (demonstrator and robot share the spawn region, objects always in the same place), a variable shared region (same region, objects in any of four locations), and different regions (demonstrator and robot operate from different spawn regions).

Ongoing activity

Proposal

VC-VLA: Video-Conditioned Vision-Language-Action

To address RQ1, we designed VC-VLA, which extends language-conditioned policies to the human-video-conditioned setting by separating task inference from task execution.

VC-VLA overview. A video-captioning module (Cosmos-Reason V2) performs task inference, turning the raw human video into a high-level textual instruction; this instruction, together with the robot's visual observation and proprioceptive state, is fed to a language-conditioned Vision-Language-Action model (task execution), evaluated here with OpenVLA-OFT, TinyVLA-H, and VLA-JEPA as interchangeable backbones. This decoupled design requires no manual segmentation, task annotation, or kinematic alignment.

  Real-World Results

To verify that these robustness properties transfer beyond simulation, a preliminary evaluation campaign was carried out on a real UR5e manipulator, reproducing the same pick-and-place scenario with 40 teleoperated demonstrations per task variation across five spatial configurations.

W.O.: Success on Wrong Object; W.B.: Success on Wrong Bin. TD: 48 trajectories (4 runs × 12 task variations); OTD: 16 trajectories (4 runs × 4 task variations). The best-performing baselines are the two methods built on hand-crafted heuristics, SeeDo and OSVI-AWDA, which transfer to the real robot almost without degradation. Fully data-driven methods (COD-Policy, Interleave-VLA, VLA-JEPA) show a pronounced sim-to-real gap: VLA-JEPA drops to 0.67 TD / 0.75 OTD Success against 0.98 / 0.97 in simulation.

OSVI-AWDA predicts attributed waypoints (Free-Space, Grasp, Carry, Drop) from the human video, which are executed through hand-crafted motor primitives.

At the grasp waypoint, a refinement step localizes the a-priori-known object in the wrist RGB-D view (contour, centroid, depth), relying on an accurate camera-to-robot calibration.

This gap has a clear explanation. SeeDo and OSVI-AWDA rely on hand-crafted heuristics that require a-priori knowledge of (i) the task, since SeeDo's prompt must be tuned per task (see the SeeDo analysis), and (ii) the object to be manipulated, which is localized through a calibrated camera-to-robot transform. Learned control policies are more flexible given enough data, but more sensitive to the sim-to-real domain gap and to the lower quality of real teleoperated demonstrations, which are noisier and costly to collect. Real-world evaluation of the proposed VC-VLA pipeline reuses the RQ1-1 train/test task configurations on the real UR5e and is currently in progress.

Appendix

SeeDo: Prompt Tuning and Calibration Robustness

SeeDo's strong real-world numbers rest on two assumptions that VC-VLA does not need: (i) a task-specific prompt to localize the target object in the robot scene, and (ii) an accurately calibrated camera-to-robot transform. The experiments below measure how sensitive SeeDo is to each.

1. Agent scene description

For the same pick-and-place task, a prior-guided prompt that restricts the detector vocabulary to the four known cube colors is compared with a generalized prompt that describes each object only by its visible color and a generic object type, with and without distractor objects in the scene.

Scene with only the four target cubes (no distractors).

Same scene with additional distractor objects.

Scene Metric Prior-guided · Class Prior-guided · Attribute Generalized · Class Generalized · Attribute
No distractors Acc. / Prec. / Rec. / F1 1.00 1.00 1.00 1.00
With distractors Accuracy 0.80 0.64 0.94 0.88
With distractors Precision 0.91 0.81 1.00 1.00
With distractors Recall 0.88 0.75 0.94 0.88
With distractors F1-score 0.89 0.78 0.97 0.93

Class: correct object type; Attribute: object type and correct color. Without distractors both prompts are perfect. With distractors, the prior-guided prompt, despite encoding stronger scene assumptions, is the more brittle one: its closed vocabulary forces the detector to read visually similar distractors as target cubes, inflating false positives. Closed-vocabulary priors help only in the exact scene they were tuned for.

2. Prompt generalization across tasks

The same generalized prompt is applied, unchanged, to two structurally different tasks: cube pick-and-place and nut assembly (pick a nut and place it onto a peg). A tuned variant then returns structured JSON records separating each object's category from its attributes, and grounds unfamiliar shapes with concrete nouns (e.g., “wooden peg”).

Cube pick-and-place: pick a cube and place it into a storage bin.

Nut assembly: pick a nut and place it onto a peg.

Prompt Box acc. Bin acc. Pick-and-place Success Nut acc. Peg acc. Nut-assembly Success
Generalized 1.00 1.00 1.00 1.00 0.00 0.00
Tuned generalized 1.00 1.00 1.00 1.00 1.00 0.00

Prompt tuning raises peg localization from 0.00 to 1.00, yet nut-assembly success stays at 0.00: the remaining gap lies in the grasping and manipulation strategy, which lacks object-specific knowledge such as the peg's pose and thread alignment. Adapting SeeDo to a new task therefore costs a round of prompt tuning plus a separate, still unresolved, adaptation of the motor-primitive library, a cost that a learned pipeline such as VC-VLA is designed to absorb from data.

3. Sensitivity to camera-calibration noise

Zero-mean Gaussian noise is added independently to each translation and rotation component of the camera-to-robot extrinsic transform; SeeDo is evaluated over 5 trials of the same pick-and-place task at each noise level.
