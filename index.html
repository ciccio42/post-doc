Overview

What this report covers

This report documents the research activity carried out during the first six-month period of the Research Grant, focused entirely on REASONED and, within it, on the currently active research question, RQ1 — the robustness of human-video-conditioned manipulation policies. RQ2 and RQ3 are planned for the following reporting periods.

RQ1 · current focus

Robustness to novel tasks

How can we develop a system that is robust to novel tasks — new combinations of skills and objects (RQ1-1), and new spatial configurations with known objects (RQ1-2)?

RQ2

Robustness to demonstrator domain shift

How can we design a system that is robust to changes in the demonstrator domain, such as different environments and viewpoints of the human video? Planned for the next period.

RQ3

Robustness to agent domain shift

How can we design a system that is robust to changes in the agent domain — workspace, camera configuration, embodiment, or sim-to-real transfer? Planned for the next period.

Main Research Project

Human-Video-Conditioned Multi-Task Imitation Learning

A fundamental goal in robotics is building autonomous systems that can perform multiple manipulation tasks in flexible, dynamic environments. Human-Video Conditioning lets a robot learn a new task simply by watching a human demonstrator perform it — a more direct and intuitive form of task specification than natural-language instructions, since a video encodes not only what has to be done but also how. The central challenge is that the human demonstration cannot be transferred to the robot directly: demonstrator and robot may differ in embodiment, object layout, viewpoint, or environment.

Problem formulation

πθ: Or × Sr × Cd → Ar

The robot policy must map its current observation (Or), its proprioceptive state (Sr), and a video demonstration provided by a human demonstrator (Cd) to a robot action (Ar). This work focuses on the most general case: the demonstration is an unstructured video, with no action labels, segmentation, or kinematic alignment between demonstrator and robot.

D1.1 Taxonomy & Baselines

Recent Human-Video Conditioning methods were organized into a taxonomy based on the information extracted from the demonstration video, and a representative baseline was selected from each family to characterize RQ1 robustness in the sections below.

Object-centric — COD-Policy

A Conditioned-Object Detector localizes the task-relevant objects from the demonstration to condition the control policy.

End-to-end — OSVI-AWDA

Predicts a sequence of attributed waypoints from the demonstration, executed via hand-crafted motor primitives.

Vision-Language-Action — OpenVLA-OFT / TinyVLA-H

Large pretrained vision-language backbones with an action head, conditioned on ground-truth language instructions (upper-bound references).

World-Action Model — VLA-JEPA

Pretrains a latent dynamics encoder on action-free video before adapting to robot control; the strongest single-model baseline observed.

COD-Policy — the Conditioned-Object Detector identifies the target object and bin from the human video demonstration.

OSVI-AWDA — a task-inference module predicts attributed waypoints, executed through hand-crafted motor primitives.

Evaluation scenario: the robot must pick a colored object and place it into the bin indicated by the human video demonstration — 16 pick-place variations in total.

RQ1 is decomposed into two robustness axes: RQ1-1, task-level (new task combinations, new object instances), and RQ1-2, spatial-level (known but different region, unseen position).

Ongoing activity

D1.3 · Proposal

VC-VLA — Video-Conditioned Vision-Language-Action

To address RQ1, we designed VC-VLA, which extends language-conditioned policies to the human-video-conditioned setting by separating task inference from task execution.

VC-VLA overview. A video-captioning module (Cosmos-Reason V2) performs task inference, turning the raw human video into a high-level textual instruction; this instruction, together with the robot's visual observation and proprioceptive state, is fed to a language-conditioned Vision-Language-Action model (task execution) — evaluated here with OpenVLA-OFT, TinyVLA-H, and VLA-JEPA as interchangeable backbones. This decoupled design requires no manual segmentation, task annotation, or kinematic alignment.

On the RQ1-1 (task-level) axis, VC-VLA is largely backbone-agnostic and remains robust OTD, with the residual gap to the language-conditioned upper bound explained almost entirely by bin-identification errors in the captioning module (Cosmos+TinyVLA-H is the exception, inheriting TinyVLA-H's own Wrong-Object failure mode). On the RQ1-2 (spatial-level) axis, preliminary Cosmos+VLA-JEPA results mirror the baseline picture: performance holds for a known-but-different region, but drops sharply for genuinely unseen positions (0.79 → 0.26) — confirming spatial-level robustness as the key open problem for this ongoing activity.

  Real-World Results

To verify that these robustness properties transfer beyond simulation, a preliminary evaluation campaign was carried out on a real UR5e manipulator, reproducing the same pick-and-place scenario with 40 teleoperated demonstrations per task variation across five spatial configurations.

Real UR5e workspace (bottom) matched against its simulated counterpart (top), with corresponding third-view and wrist-camera viewpoints.

Data-collection protocol: five spatial configurations of the target objects relative to the fixed bin layout, used to diversify the teleoperated demonstrations.

Besides the learned baselines already used in simulation, the real-world campaign also includes SeeDo, a non-learning-based baseline that grounds a demonstration through hand-crafted heuristics and pretrained Vision-Language Models rather than a learned policy.

SeeDo pipeline: keyframe selection and object tracking on the demonstration video produce a symbolic plan (VLM interpreter), which is grounded at runtime and compiled into executable robot code.

Hand-crafted, heuristic-based methods (SeeDo, OSVI-AWDA) transfer to the real robot almost without degradation, at the cost of requiring a-priori task and object knowledge and accurate camera-to-robot calibration. Purely learned policies (e.g., VLA-JEPA), more flexible in simulation, show a larger sim-to-real gap, mainly due to noisier teleoperated demonstrations. Real-world evaluation of the proposed VC-VLA pipeline is currently in progress and will be reported in the next period.
